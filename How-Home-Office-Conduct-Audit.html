Direct Answer • How the Home Office Conducts an Audit

How Does the Home Office Conduct a Sponsor Licence Compliance Audit?

The Home Office conducts a sponsor licence audit (officially termed a compliance visit or digital compliance inspection) by evaluating whether a licensed sponsor is fulfilling its legal duties to prevent illegal working and maintain accurate sponsorship governance. Audits can be announced or unannounced and take place either on-site at your operating premises or via a remote digital audit.

Before an officer ever steps through your door, the Home Office conducts intelligence-led pre-audit checks—cross-referencing your Sponsor Management System (SMS) Certificates of Sponsorship (CoS) against real-time HMRC payroll (RTI) submissions, Companies House filings, and sector regulator reports (such as the Care Quality Commission). During the inspection itself, Home Office compliance officers audit your Right to Work checks, verify Appendix D employee records, test period-by-period salary compliance, inspect shift rota and attendance logs, and conduct live interviews with your Authorising Officer (AO) and a sample of sponsored workers.

Notice Period 0 Days (Unannounced) or 7–14 Days

Refusing entry to an unannounced Home Office compliance officer results in immediate licence non-compliance.

Inside the Inspection Process

Step-by-Step: The 5 Stages of a Home Office Compliance Audit

Understanding the exact methodology used by Home Office compliance officers reveals why last-minute preparation is rarely effective.

1

Stage 1: Pre-Visit Intelligence & HMRC Data Cross-Referencing

Before You Are Contacted

Modern Home Office audits begin long before an inspection letter is sent or an officer knocks on the door. The Home Office systematically cross-references your Sponsor Management System (SMS) data against HMRC Real Time Information (RTI) payroll feeds. If a sponsored worker’s Certificate of Sponsorship (CoS) states they earn £29,000 per year, yet HMRC RTI data shows their gross pay dipped below the monthly threshold in a single pay period, an automated compliance flag is raised.

2

Stage 2: Arrival (Announced vs. Unannounced) & Key Personnel Verification

On-Site or Digital Demand

During an on-site visit, Home Office compliance officers arrive at the addresses listed on your sponsor licence. They verify that the premises are genuine, operational, and match the locations reported on the SMS. They immediately ask to meet the Authorising Officer (AO) and Level 1 User to assess whether the individuals named on the licence actually exercise day-to-day oversight of sponsorship governance.

3

Stage 3: Forensic HR, Payroll & Appendix D Document Examination

Core Evidence Review

Officers select a sample—or in smaller care providers, 100%—of sponsored and non-sponsored employee files. They verify statutory Right to Work (RTW) checks (including dated digital eVisa share-code verification logs), recruitment records, signed employment contracts, job descriptions, professional registrations, and itemised payslips alongside bank transfer proofs for every single pay period.

4

Stage 4: Live Interviews with the Authorising Officer & Sponsored Workers

Verbal Cross-Checking

Compliance officers interview sponsored workers privately to confirm their actual day-to-day duties, working hours, care home or domiciliary routes, salary received, and whether any unlawful fees or deductions were charged. If a worker’s testimony contradicts what is recorded on their CoS or rota, the Home Office treats this as a serious breach of sponsorship duties.

5

Stage 5: Post-Audit Compliance Report & Licence Decision

Regulatory Determination

Following the audit, the compliance officer compiles an internal report for the Home Office casework team. Outcomes range from maintaining your A-rating, downgrading to a B-rating with a paid time-limited Action Plan, immediate Sponsor Licence Suspension (with only 20 working days to submit legal and factual representations), or outright Sponsor Licence Revocation—which curtails the visas of all your sponsored staff.

Beyond Recruitment Onboarding

Why Providers Need Home Office Mock Audits

Many organisations assume they are compliant because they completed right-to-work checks when employees first joined. However, sponsor compliance extends far beyond recruitment.

Sponsors must continue to meet ongoing obligations throughout the entire lifecycle of sponsorship—including maintaining accurate Appendix D records, reporting relevant changes through the Sponsor Management System (SMS), monitoring sponsored workers’ attendance and role duties, and ensuring employment and salary arrangements remain compliant every single month.

Even well-managed organisations can develop compliance gaps if processes are not regularly reviewed—particularly when care managers change, new branches open, or shift patterns fluctuate. A Home Office Mock Audit provides a structured opportunity to test whether those systems work effectively in practice before a compliance officer arrives.

Common Sponsor Misconceptions

❌ Myth: "We did the Right to Work check at onboarding, so we're safe." Reality: Onboarding is only 15% of an audit. The Home Office revokes licences over unreported location changes, role drift, and monthly payroll dips.
❌ Myth: "We will tidy our files when the Home Office announces a visit." Reality: Many visits are unannounced, and historic HMRC payroll or SMS late-reporting footprints cannot be retroactively erased.
Operational & Regulatory Risk Analysis

The Cost of Prevention Is Almost Always Lower Than the Cost of Enforcement

One of the most common misconceptions among sponsor licence holders is that compliance only becomes a priority when the Home Office announces a compliance visit. By that stage, however, it may already be too late to address issues quickly and easily.

The Home Office has significantly increased its enforcement activity against sponsor licence holders. Between October 2025 and June 2026, 8,301 Skilled Worker sponsor licences were revoked and suspended—the highest ever since the sponsor licence system was introduced in 2008. This highlights the increasingly active compliance environment facing sponsors, with the Home Office using employer data, payroll information, and intelligence-led checks to identify potential breaches.

For care providers, the implications are particularly significant. A sponsor licence suspension or revocation is not simply a regulatory issue; it can create immediate operational challenges. Organisations may face disruption to recruitment, acute uncertainty for sponsored care workers, safe-staffing shortfalls across care homes or domiciliary routes, additional pressure on registered managers, and the need to respond urgently to complex compliance concerns.

The important point for providers is that compliance problems are far easier to identify and resolve before they become enforcement issues. Addressing weaknesses while they are still manageable allows organisations to strengthen their systems before regulatory scrutiny begins.

Prevention is not just about avoiding penalties. It is about protecting workforce stability, reducing operational risk, and ensuring that compliance systems work effectively in practice. Addressing gaps before a compliance visit is significantly less disruptive, less stressful, and more cost-effective than trying to resolve problems after enforcement action has already begun.

2026 Regulatory Deep Dive

Tightened Rules in 2026: Pay-Period Thresholds & Section 48 Duties

The Home Office has tightened the rules around sponsor compliance in parallel with rising enforcement. Two landmark 2026 reforms have fundamentally changed what compliant HR and payroll look like.

In Force Since 8 April 2026

Mandatory Salary Compliance Across Each Individual Pay Period

Since the 8th of April 2026, sponsors must show that a worker’s salary meets the required threshold not only as an annual figure, but across each individual pay period. A worker whose pay dips below the threshold in a single month, even temporarily, can now put a sponsor in breach of their licence duties.

For care providers managing variable shifts, overtime, and fluctuating hours, this changes what compliant payroll actually looks like. It is no longer enough to show that a worker was paid correctly across the year.

Sponsors need records that demonstrate compliance period by period, and HR and payroll teams need to understand exactly how those periods are assessed before HMRC Real Time Information (RTI) data flags a discrepancy to the Home Office.

Care Sector Example: If a sponsored Senior Care Worker takes 4 days of unauthorised or unpaid leave in a single month without a valid statutory exception, causing that month's gross pay to drop below their required monthly floor, automated HMRC data matching can trigger a Home Office compliance audit.
In Force Since 1 October 2026

Section 48 (Border Security, Asylum & Immigration Act 2025) & HMRC Matching

Sponsor licence revocations increased sharply in 2025 and 2026, and the Home Office continues to expand its use of HMRC payroll data, cross-referencing employer records and other intelligence sources to identify sponsors whose figures do not add up.

Right to work obligations have changed too. Section 48 of the Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act 2025 came into force on 1 October 2026, expanding scrutiny over working arrangements and right-to-work verification.

Providers must treat immigration compliance as an active, ongoing project rather than something to revisit only when a Home Office audit is announced.

Action Required for Employers: Audit all existing workers, variable-hour staff, bank/agency arrangements, and time-limited eVisa expiry calendars to ensure complete alignment with Section 48 standards.
